The interviewer is not looking for silence followed by perfect code. They need a clear view of how you analyse, choose, test, and adapt.

Make your reasoning legible

Before coding

Set the agenda out loud

Try: “I’ll confirm assumptions, work through an example, describe a baseline, then optimise before implementing.” This makes the conversation collaborative and buys time to think.

During coding

Narrate decisions, not every keystroke

Explain invariants, variables, and meaningful branches. Pause when a requirement changes. Use clear function names and small helper methods when they make the logic easier to verify.

After coding

Close the loop

Test with an example, state complexity, then offer a possible extension. Do not claim a solution is optimal unless you can explain why a better bound is unlikely.

What to do when stuck

Recover productively

First, say what you know: inputs, output, a small example, and a working baseline. Then ask a targeted question or propose a constrained next step. Avoid guessing silently for several minutes.

Useful recovery language “The brute-force version is clear and runs in O(n²). I suspect we can avoid revisiting earlier values with a map. May I explore that approach?” “I am deciding between BFS and DFS. Since we need the fewest edges in an unweighted graph, I will choose BFS and keep a visited set.”
Remember: an interviewer can often help with a prompt, but only if you expose where your uncertainty is. Asking a precise question is a strength.

Behavioural answers: STAR + reflection

Structure a concise, evidence-based story

Situation: enough context to understand the stakes. Task: your responsibility. Action: what you personally did and why. Result: measurable outcome. Finish with Reflection: what you learned or would change.

Prompt: Tell me about a disagreement. Situation: Two engineers disagreed on a migration plan. Action: I wrote down latency, risk, and rollout criteria; we prototyped both paths. Result: We chose a staged migration and avoided a planned outage. Reflection: Shared decision criteria turned preference into a testable choice.

Practice prompts

  1. Clarification: receive “find the most frequent item” and list five assumptions you would confirm.
  2. Think aloud: explain how you would find a cycle in a linked list, without writing code.
  3. Recovery: practise a 30-second response after realising your first approach is too slow.
  4. STAR: prepare stories for conflict, failure, ownership, ambiguity, and learning something quickly.
